# William Playfair

**What they are known for.** *The Commercial and Political Atlas* (1786) and *The Statistical Breviary* (1801), which between them introduced the statistical time-series line chart, the bar chart and the pie chart. He built all three to win arguments about trade and taxation, not to analyze anything.

**How this was read.** Full text of two *Atlas* editions from the Internet Archive: the 1801 third edition for the introduction, the 1787 second for the plate commentaries. The OCR renders the long s as `f`. Quotes are silently de-long-s'd and otherwise verbatim. Three plates were examined at full resolution from Wikimedia Commons scans: Plate 1 of the 1786 *Atlas* (imports and exports of England), the Scotland bar chart from the same volume, and the *Breviary* comparison plate in its French edition. All public domain.

**What they are good for.** What a figure that argues looked like before anyone called it bad taste, with the inventor's own reasoning and doubts recorded beside the charts.

**What they do not settle.** Whether any of it works. His central claim, that the eye judges proportion better than any other organ, is `authority-asserted`. He never tested it, and the parts later tested came out mixed.

## "In the style of Playfair" means, concretely

**Palette.** Hand-colored copperplate on cream laid paper: crimson exports, ochre imports and a pale blue wash between them on Plate 1, pale green, pink and yellow washes on the *Breviary*. Color is a wash over a black line, never the mark itself.

**Frame and grid.** A heavy black frame around a full-strength horizontal and vertical grid. Plate 1's vertical rules tighten after 1770 as the data goes annual.

**Axes.** Time runs along the bottom, outside the frame. Plate 1's value scale runs down the right edge (`Millions`, `£`), the *Breviary*'s down both edges, and the bar chart's along the top with categories on the right.

**Labels on the mark, no legend.** Plate 1 letters `Line Representing Exports` and `Line Representing Imports into England` along the curves, and stamps `BALLANCE IN FAVOUR OF ENGLAND` through the fill between them.

**A footer that explains the encoding.** Plate 1: *"The Divisions at the Bottom, express YEARS, & those on the Right hand, MILLIONS of POUNDS."* The bar chart: *"The Upright divisions are Ten Thousand Pounds each. The Black Lines are Exports the Ribbed Lines Imports."*

**Texture for categories.** The bar chart pairs a solid black bar with a hatched, "ribbed" one that survives uncolored printing, [Bertin's `grain`](jacques-bertin.md) a century and a half early. Bars are sorted with the smallest at the top, under a right-hand column headed `Names of Places.`

**Furniture.** Engraved titles and subtitle, plate number (`Pl. 25`), engraver (`J. Ainslie Sculp.`, `Gravé par Adam`), publication line (`Published as the Act directs, 20th Augst 1785`) and page cross-reference (`to Face Page 3d`).

**Zero baselines, and a compound plate.** Baselines are always at zero. On the *Breviary* each nation is a circle whose area is its territory, flanked by vertical lines for population and revenue joined by a dashed line. Where it rises left to right, the nation is taxed above its population.

## What they established, and what gets over-claimed in their name

### He claimed priority for finance, not for the method

From the 1801 introduction:

> "The studies of history, genealogy, and chronology have been much improved by copper-plate charts. It is now sixteen years since I first thought of applying lines, to subjects of Finance."

> "I confess I was long anxious to find out, whether I was actually the first who applied the principles of geometry to matters of Finance, as it had long before been applied to chronology with great success. I am now satisfied, upon due inquiry, that I was the first; for during fifteen years I have not been able to learn that any thing of a similar nature had ever before been produced."

So "Playfair invented the line chart" is wrong by his own account: time charts were established in chronology (Priestley's timelines and their relatives). His claim, which holds, is the application to economic quantities.

### He invented the bar chart reluctantly and called it inferior

The Scotland plate, the first known bar chart, has a commentary in the 1786 volume that opens by apologizing:

> "This Chart is different from the others in principle, as it does not comprehend any portion of time, and it is much inferior in utility [to] charts that do; for though it gives the extent of the different branches of trade, it does not compare the same branch of commerce with itself at different periods; nor does it imprint upon the mind that distinct idea, in doing which, the chief advantage of Charts consists; for as it wants the dimension that is formed by duration, there is no shape given to the quantities."

He had one year of Scottish data and no series, said so in the first sentence of the commentary, and treated the chart as a degraded fallback.

### The first pie is part of a compound plate

It is in the *Breviary*, not the *Atlas*: one circle on the 1801 comparison plate, the Turkish Empire sliced into Asian, European and African parts, in a row of unsliced circles. A lone pie reproduced as "Playfair's first pie chart" is a crop.

### The charts are rhetoric aimed at tax policy

His stated goal is attention, not accuracy:

> "The human mind has been so acted upon for a number of years past, and the same subjects have been so frequently brought forward, that it is necessary to produce novelty, but above all to aim at facility, in communicating information; for the desire of obtaining it has diminished in proportion as disgust and satiety have increased."

The *Breviary*'s population-versus-revenue plate argues that the British are overtaxed relative to other nations. [Hugh Small](https://www.york.ac.uk/depts/maths/histstat/small.htm), in the Nightingale literature, uses Playfair as the prescriptive-but-not-causal counterexample: the graphics compare, and leave open what would follow from changing anything.

### The famous sentence is an assertion, since carved up

> "As the eye is the best judge of proportion, being able to estimate it with more quickness and accuracy than any other of our organs, it follows, that wherever relative quantities are in question ... this mode of representing it is peculiarly applicable"

[Cleveland & McGill](../studies/cleveland-mcgill-1984.md) later measured the parts of "the eye" he meant. His line and bar charts use position along a common scale, the best-read channel in both their tables. His circles use area, well below it, and [Heer & Bostock](../studies/heer-bostock-2010.md) later found area about as bad whether circular or rectangular, with squares the worst case.

### A contemporary review he answered

The 1801 introduction reprints a *Political Herald* review, criticism included:

> "although we would recommend to the author to do whatever he can, in any future editions, to make his leading ideas as familiar as possible to every imagination, by additional illustrations and directions; for these in some instances, seem to be wanting."

His footnote reply: *"This last stricture is certainly just; and I have attended to the hint."* The plate footers are the response.

### The biography behind "father of statistical graphics"

Playfair was a serial and sometimes fraudulent speculator, per the standard modern lives (Spence, Berkowitz). That is `secondary-only`, from general reference reading. It is context for the work, not an argument against it: the charts were built to persuade by a man whose profession was persuading people about money.

## Works, and where they sit in this wiki

- ***The Commercial and Political Atlas*** (1786; 2nd ed. 1787; 3rd ed. 1801). No source page. The second and third editions were read.
- ***The Statistical Breviary*** (1801), with the first pie chart. Only the plate was read, in French, not the text.
- ***An Inquiry into the Permanent Causes of the Decline and Fall of Powerful and Wealthy Nations*** (1805), source of the wheat-and-wages chart Tufte reproduces. Not reached.
