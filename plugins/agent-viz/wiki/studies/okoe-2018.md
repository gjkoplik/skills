# Okoe, Jianu & Kobourov (2018/2019), *Node-Link or Adjacency Matrices*

Mmumbi Okoe, Radu Jianu and Stephen Kobourov, "Node-Link or Adjacency Matrices: Old Question, New Insights," *IEEE TVCG* 25(10), 2019, pp. 2940-2952. Expanded from a Graph Drawing 2017 paper. A large crowdsourced re-examination of the question [Ghoniem et al.](ghoniem-2004.md) answered in 2004.

**What it is good for.** The counter-evidence that keeps "matrices beat node-link above twenty nodes" from being stated flatly.

**What it does not settle.** The general question. The authors limit their findings to networks like theirs with an interaction set like theirs.

**How this was read.** The accepted manuscript, not the published version, from the City, University of London open-access repository, text re-extracted locally.

## What changed from 2004

| | Ghoniem et al. (2004) | Okoe et al. (2018) |
|---|---|---|
| Networks | Random, 20-100 nodes, density 0.2-0.6 | Two real networks: 256 nodes / 1,090 edges, and 332 nodes / 2,126 edges |
| Display | Static | Interactive |
| Tasks | 7 | 14, spanning multiple task taxonomies |
| Participants | Lab, volunteers | 864 online |

Networks, display and tasks all changed, and all three plausibly drive the different answer. The authors put it as "Ghoniem et al. evaluated random networks with fewer nodes, albeit somewhat denser." Their networks are in fact larger *and* roughly an order of magnitude sparser, the regime most real networks occupy.

## Results

From the introduction:

> "NL outperforms AM for questions about graph topology (e.g., 'Select all neighbors of node,' 'Is a highlighted node connected to a named node?'). Of 10 such tasks in two datasets, participants who used the node-link diagram were more accurate in 5 and less accurate in 3. AM outperforms NL in 2 of 4 tasks which tested the ability of the participants to identify and compare node groups or clusters. Finally, NL has a slight edge on 1 of 2 memorability tasks."

From the conclusion:

> "We found that NL is better than AM for questions about network topology, connectivity, and memorability tasks, while AM outperforms NL for group tasks. These findings apply to visualizing datasets similar to the ones we evaluated, provided a similar interaction set."

"NL outperforms AM for topology" summarizes 5 wins, 3 losses and 2 ties out of 10, a modest effect. Matrices win 2 of 4 group tasks. Neither representation sweeps.

## Reconciling the two studies

The two studies do not contradict each other, and reading one as refuting the other misreports both.

- **Density.** Ghoniem tested 0.2 to 0.6. Okoe's networks sit near 0.03. Node-link's failure mode is edge occlusion, which grows with density. At Ghoniem's densities a 100-node node-link diagram is a hairball. At Okoe's it is legible.
- **Interaction.** Ghoniem's displays were static, and that paper's conclusion asks for interaction on path tasks. Okoe supplied it. Interaction disproportionately helps node-link, because it fixes occlusion and tracing.
- **Task set.** Okoe's 14 tasks include grouping and memorability, which 2004 did not test. Matrices win the grouping tasks, consistent with the 2004 finding that ordered matrices support structure reading.

Taken together: matrices are favored by density and by group-structure tasks, and node-link by sparsity, interaction, and topology and path tasks. Size alone, the variable the 2004 result is usually quoted on, does not decide it.

## Limits the authors state

- Results apply to "the specific underlying networks and the specific implementations of NL and AM visualizations."
- Some participants reported insufficient time. The authors argue both conditions were affected equally, so comparisons are likely unaffected.
- Two networks, both real.

## Evidence class

- **Evidence-backed, within scope.** On sparse real-world networks of a few hundred nodes with interaction, node-link is better for topology, connectivity and memorability, and matrices for group and cluster tasks.
- **Contested, jointly with 2004.** Any general "X beats Y for large graphs" claim. Together the two studies support a *conditional* answer and refute the unconditional one.
